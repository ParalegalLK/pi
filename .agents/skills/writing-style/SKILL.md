# Writing Style

Prose style rules for all drafted documents. These rules govern language only, not document structure or formatting, which are owned by the calling skill.

## 1. Sentence Construction

### Active voice
Use active voice. Within reported-speech frames ("The Petitioner states that..."), the substance must still use active voice.

- "The unions launched a six-hour token strike" not "A six-hour token strike was launched"
- "The Act makes the gazetting irreversible" not "The gazetting is irreversible"
- "The successor entities have negotiated no terms" not "No terms have been negotiated or agreed between the successor entities"

### Positive form
Make definite assertions. Do not use "not" as a means of evasion.

- "dishonest" not "not honest"
- "undermines the viability" not "has a direct bearing on the viability"

### Parallel construction
Express coordinate ideas in similar form. If a list begins "X will [verb] but will have no [noun]," every item in that list must follow the same grammatical pattern.

### Emphasis at the end
Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end. End on the concrete, not the abstract.

- "only fresh legislation enacted by Parliament can restore the CEB and the regulatory framework" not "...can restore the status quo ante"

## 2. Conciseness

### Omit needless words
A sentence should contain no unnecessary words. A paragraph should contain no unnecessary sentences.

### Establish-once rule
When a phrase or concept is introduced and explained (e.g., "by operation of law," "on the appointed date"), do not repeat the explanation. State it once with precision, then use it without re-establishing.

### No doublets
Use one word where legal habit uses two synonyms joined by "and."

- "part hereof" not "part and parcel hereof"
- "irreparable harm" not "grave and irreparable harm and damage"
- "plans and strategies" not "plans, proposals and strategies" (unless each word does distinct work)

### Kill qualifiers
Words like "rather," "very," "significant," "quite," and "somewhat" weaken the sentence. Cut them unless they are quantified or essential.

- "negative feedback" not "significant negative feedback" (unless you state from whom and how many)
- "undermines" not "directly undermines" (the verb is already strong)

## 3. Punctuation

### Dashes: use sparingly
Em dashes are a giveaway of AI-generated text when overused. Default to other punctuation. Use dashes only when no other mark works cleanly.

**In headings:** use a colon.
- "No Financial Plan: Section 18(2)(a)" not "No Financial Plan — Section 18(2)(a)"

**For parenthetical insertions:** use commas.
- "the CEB, which has operated since 1969, shall cease to exist" not "the CEB — which has operated since 1969 — shall cease to exist"

**When the parenthetical contains internal commas:** use parentheses to avoid ambiguity.
- "In an emergency (a generating unit failure, a transmission fault, or a frequency disturbance) the response requires..." not "In an emergency — a generating unit failure, a transmission fault, or a frequency disturbance — the response requires..."

**For contrast or a sharp turn:** use a semicolon.
- "does not merely render the Order ultra vires; it revives the constitutional inconsistency" not "does not merely render the Order ultra vires — it revives..."

**For amplification after an independent clause:** use a colon.

**The only acceptable dash uses:** a genuinely abrupt interruption mid-sentence, or a summary after a list. If you find more than two dashes in a full document, review whether each is necessary.

### Serial comma
Use a comma after each term except the last in a series of three or more.

### Joining clauses
Comma before a conjunction (and, but, for, or, nor) introducing an independent clause. Semicolon between independent clauses with no conjunction.

### Colons
Use a colon after an independent clause to introduce a list, quotation, or amplification. Do not use a colon after a sentence fragment.

### Enumerated lists
When a sentence breaks into enumerated items (whether lettered or bulleted), the introductory clause and the items together form a single sentence. Punctuate accordingly:

- Each item except the last two terminates with a semicolon.
- The penultimate item terminates with "; and" (no semicolon after "and").
- The final item terminates with a full stop.

Correct:

> The NSO shall enter into:
>
> (a) power purchase agreements with generation licensees (section 10(14)(b));
> (b) power sales agreements with distribution licensees (section 10(14)(c)); and
> (c) a transmission service agreement with the NTNSP (section 10(14)(d)).

Wrong: "; and;" on the penultimate line (double punctuation). Wrong: a semicolon after the final item instead of a full stop. Wrong: no punctuation at all between items.

## 4. Repetition Across Sections

### Reference, do not restate
If a point is established in one section, later sections must not repeat the same facts at the same level of detail. Instead, reference the earlier section and state the consequence or new angle.

- "As set out above, no inter-company agreements exist between the successor entities. From the day following the appointed date, electricity will be generated without a contractual basis for payment at any link in the chain."
- Not: re-listing all five missing agreements that were already enumerated three sections earlier.

### Each section must earn its place
Every section must add something new: a new fact, a new legal argument, or a new consequence. If a section merely summarises what came before, merge it or differentiate it.

## 5. Objectivity

### No editorialising
State facts and let the reader draw conclusions. Do not use adjectives that amount to the conclusion you are arguing for.

- "Unbundling the CEB on the basis of the PTP marked P1" not "Premature unbundling on the basis of a deficient PTP"
- The facts should make the case for "premature" and "deficient"; the drafter should not say so.

### Do not overstate
Overstatement causes the reader to lose confidence in your judgment. If a claim is strong, state it plainly. If it is weak, do not dress it up.

## 6. Word Choice

### Specific and concrete
Prefer the specific to the general, the concrete to the abstract.

- "the inter-company agreements, asset valuations, financial plans, and staffing structures" not "all material substance"
- Name the things. Do not gesture at them.

### Nouns and verbs
Write with nouns and verbs. Adjectives and adverbs rarely add force.

### Plain words
Do not use an elaborate word when a plain one will do. Do not sprinkle foreign expressions unless they are established terms of art in the relevant field.

### Avoid fancy words
"use" not "utilise." "begin" not "commence" (unless "commence" is a statutory term). "about" not "approximately" (unless precision matters). "because" not "due to the fact that."
